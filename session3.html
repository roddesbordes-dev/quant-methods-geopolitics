Who depends on whom?

Economic security has become a policy field of its own: the European Commission, NATO and every finance ministry now track dependencies. In this session you measure dependence the way they do, build a vulnerability index, and see how easily an index can be bent.

    Step 2 · Lesson · 25 min

    Measuring dependence

    Import shares

    The simplest measure: what share of a product's imports comes from one supplier. The European Commission flags a product as a dependency when one or a few suppliers dominate and the EU cannot easily replace them.

    Concentration: the Herfindahl index

    A 50% share from one supplier is risky if the other 50% comes from one more country, much less so if it comes from twenty. The Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) captures this: square each supplier's share (in %) and add them up.

    HHI = s₁² + s₂² + s₃² + … (from near 0 to 10,000)

    One supplier with 100%: 10,000. Two suppliers with 50% each: 2,500 + 2,500 = 5,000. Ten suppliers with 10% each: 1,000. Competition authorities in the United States treat markets above roughly 1,800 to 2,500 as highly concentrated; trade analysts borrow the same scale.

    Can the supplier be replaced?

    Shares and concentration describe the past. What matters in a crisis is substitutability: whether other producers exist, how long it takes to switch, and whether stocks can bridge the gap.

    The Lithuanian case

    In 2021 Lithuania let Taiwan open a representative office under the name “Taiwanese” rather than “Taipei”. China retaliated. On paper, Lithuania was hardly exposed: China took 1.1% of its exports in 2020. Yet the pressure hurt, because China also blocked goods from other EU countries that contained Lithuanian parts, and German firms with plants in Lithuania warned they might leave (Global Taiwan Institute, 2022). Lithuanian exports to China fell to 0.2% of its total by 2022.

    Check yourself

    Three suppliers with 60%, 30% and 10%. What is the HHI?

    Step 4 · Feedback · 10 min

    Concentrated and hard to replace

    Solar panels, magnesium, permanent magnets and laptops all have HHIs above 7,000: one supplier, China, dominates. Crude oil is the opposite: the EU buys from many countries, so its HHI is below 1,000, even though Europe imports almost all its oil. Liquefied natural gas sits in between, with the United States now the largest supplier.

    Which of these numbers would have warned Lithuania? None. Export shares measured direct trade with China, and they were tiny. The exposure ran through supply chains: Lithuanian parts inside German goods sold in China, and Chinese inputs inside Lithuanian factories. Dependence measures need to follow inputs, not just final trade.

    Remember

    Share, concentration and substitutability are three different questions. A high share from a diversified, friendly market is not a vulnerability; a modest share of an irreplaceable input can be.
    Step 5 · Lesson · 25 min

    Building an index and mapping it

    Think tanks and international organisations love composite indices: one number per country, a ranking, a map. Building one takes four steps, and each hides a choice.

    1. Choose indicators. Here: the share of imports from China, the concentration (HHI) of import partners, and dependence on imported energy.
    2. Normalise them to a common scale, for example 0 for the lowest country and 100 for the highest.
    3. Weight them. Equal weights are a choice too.
    4. Aggregate and rank.

    score = w₁ × norm(China share) + w₂ × norm(HHI) + w₃ × norm(energy dependence)

    Rules for a map

    • Map rates or shares, never totals: big countries are not more vulnerable for being big.
    • Use one colour that darkens with the value, and print the numbers where you can.
    • Say what is missing; a blank country is not a zero.

    The map in the next step is a tile map: each country is one equal square placed roughly where it lies, so Malta counts as much as France. Newspapers use them for exactly this reason.

    Step 7 · Feedback · 10 min

    Same data, opposite rankings

    Most countries can be moved a long way up or down by weights alone. That is why serious indices publish their weights and show how rankings change when the weights change.

    The real case: Doing Business

    For years the World Bank's Doing Business report ranked countries on how easy it was to run a firm, and governments set targets to climb it. In 2021 an external investigation by the law firm WilmerHale found that, for the 2018 edition, changes to three data points had moved China from 85th to 78th. The World Bank discontinued the report in September 2021.

    Remember

    Before quoting a ranking, ask what went in, how it was weighted, and how much the order changes with other reasonable choices.
