Can we trust a poll?

Polls tell governments, investors and diplomats what a country thinks. In this session you recount the polls before Moldova's 2024 referendum on the European Union, learn what a margin of error does and does not cover, and decide which changes in European views of China are real.

    Step 2 · Lesson · 25 min

    How sure is a poll?

    A sample stands in for a population

    A pollster cannot ask three million voters, so it asks about a thousand chosen at random and treats their answers as a scale model of the country. Another random thousand would give slightly different answers. The margin of error measures how much the result would move from one random sample to the next.

    margin of error (95%) ≈ 1.96 × √( p × (1 − p) ÷ n )

    Here p is the share answering Yes (as a proportion, 0.55 for 55%) and n the number of people asked. In 19 samples out of 20, the true share lies within that distance of the estimate. A handy rule: with 1,000 people the margin is about ±3 points; to halve it, you need four times as many people.

    Try it

    What the margin of error does not cover

    • Who refuses to answer. If supporters of one side hang up more often, every sample is biased in the same direction, and a bigger sample does not help.
    • Who is not in the sample at all. A poll of residents misses voters abroad.
    • The undecided. How they finally vote is a guess, and the guess is yours, not the pollster's.
    • Late changes, and pressure on voters on the day.

    These are non-sampling errors. They are often larger than the margin of error, and no formula measures them.

    A poll of 1,100 people finds 50% Yes. What is the margin of error, in points? (One decimal.)

    A miss far larger than the margin

    Reveal the result in step 3 to unlock this feedback.

    50.35%Yes, all votes
    45.4%Yes, voters in Moldova
    about 77%Yes, voters abroad

    Counting decided voters only, the three polls put Yes between 59% and 68%, with margins of about ±3 points. Among voters inside Moldova, the people the polls actually sampled, Yes got 45.4%. That is a miss of 13 to 22 points, four to seven times the margin of error. Only if every undecided voter chose No do two of the polls come close. The referendum passed because of the diaspora, which pollsters did not survey.

    Why did the polls miss? Several explanations were discussed after the vote, none measured by any margin of error:

    • Undecided voters broke heavily for No.
    • Some No voters may not have told pollsters their intention.
    • Vote buying: Moldovan police documented hundreds of cases, and President Sandu alleged a scheme aimed at buying up to 300,000 votes (Corman, Verfassungsblog, 2024).

    Remember

    A margin of error measures the luck of the sample. It says nothing about who was left out, who refused, or what the undecided will do.
    Step 5 · Lesson · 25 min

    Comparing groups in a survey

    Most questions about opinion are comparisons: this year against last, young against old, France against Spain. Each number has its own margin of error, so the difference between two numbers is even less certain than either one.

    margin of a difference ≈ 1.96 × √( p₁(1 − p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1 − p₂)/n₂ )

    With about 1,000 people in each sample and shares around 30%, a difference must exceed about 4 points before you can call it real. Pew Research Center's samples are not pure random draws, so its own published margins are somewhat wider still.

    Filtering and recoding

    To compare groups you filter (keep only the rows you need, such as one country or one age group) and recode (merge categories, such as “very” and “somewhat” favourable into one “favourable”). Pew reports favourable views this way: the two positive answers added together.

    Attention is not opinion

    Google Trends measures what people search for, scaled from 0 to 100 relative to the busiest week. A spike in searches for “Taiwan” means attention, not support or hostility. Commentators often confuse the two.

    Step 7 · Feedback · 10 min

    A real warming, with exceptions

    Views of China became more favourable almost everywhere in Europe between 2024 and 2025, and in most countries the change is larger than the noise. Spain's 4-point rise is not: it is within the margin. Comparisons between countries need the same care: Spain (37%) and France (36%) are statistically the same, Italy (45%) and Germany (29%) are not.

    Inside countries, Pew found large age gaps: in Poland and the United Kingdom, adults under 35 were 27 to 28 points more likely than those aged 50 and over to view China favourably. A national average can hide two very different countries.
