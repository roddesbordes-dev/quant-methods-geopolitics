Why did German cars flood into Kyrgyzstan?

Do sanctions work, or do goods simply take a detour? International organisations answer such questions with policy evaluation. In this session you estimate how much EU trade with Russia was rerouted through its neighbours, using difference-in-differences and an event study on monthly Eurostat data.

    Step 1 · Puzzle · 10 min

    The chart

    German exports of cars and car parts to Kyrgyzstan, a country of seven million people bordering Kazakhstan, rose about sixtyfold between 2021 and 2023. Kyrgyzstan belongs to a customs union with Russia. Proof that sanctions fail?

    Your guess

    The EU's exports to Russia collapsed after February 2022. Roughly what share of the lost exports came back through Armenia, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan?

    Step 2 · Lesson · 25 min

    What would have happened anyway?

    Every evaluation compares what happened with what would have happened without the policy: the counterfactual. We never observe it, so we build it.

    Before and after is not enough

    EU exports to Russia's neighbours more than doubled after 2022. But EU exports to the rest of the world also grew, with inflation and the post-COVID recovery. A before/after comparison mixes the effect of sanctions with everything else that changed.

    Difference-in-differences

    Take a treated group (Russia's neighbours) and a comparison group exposed to the same general shocks but not to the detour (the rest of the world, excluding Russia, its other neighbours and known trade hubs). Compute the change for each and subtract:

    effect = (change for neighbours) − (change for comparison group)

    The comparison group's change is our estimate of what would have happened to the neighbours anyway. We use percentage changes here because the two groups differ enormously in size.

    The key assumption: parallel trends

    The method assumes that, without sanctions, the two groups would have moved in parallel. You cannot test it after the event, but you can check whether they moved in parallel before it. That is the job of the event study in step 5.

    Check yourself

    A treated group grows by 50%, the comparison group by 20%. What is the difference-in-differences estimate, in percentage points?

    Step 4 · Feedback · 10 min

    About a tenth came back

    Complete the calculation in step 3 to unlock this feedback.

    Had the three neighbours' imports from the EU grown like the rest of the world's (+33%), they would have averaged about €770 million a month after March 2022. They averaged €1,334 million: an extra €564 million a month, or roughly €6.8 billion a year.

    Over the same period, EU exports to Russia fell from €7.2 billion to €3.3 billion a month, while the comparison group grew by a third. Measured the same way, about €6.3 billion a month of exports to Russia were lost. The detour through Armenia, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan therefore made up roughly 9% of the loss. Real, large for those three economies, and small against the collapse.

    In euros

    A difference in percentages becomes a policy number once you translate it back into money: the counterfactual level, the excess, the share of the total.
    Step 5 · Lesson · 25 min

    Month by month

    The event study

    Instead of one before and one after, plot the ratio of the treated group to the comparison group for every month, scaled so that the pre-sanctions average equals 100. Three things to look for:

    • Before the event, the line should be flat around 100: that supports parallel trends.
    • At the event, a jump that starts when the policy starts, not before.
    • After, whether the effect persists, grows or fades.

    The placebo test

    Pretend the event happened at a date when nothing happened, say March 2020, and rerun the comparison. A large “effect” at a fake date warns that something other than the policy moves the data. Here, the pandemic is that something: trade with small neighbours fell faster than the rest in 2020–21.

    What could make the estimate wrong

    • The comparison group may have been hit by other shocks, such as the energy crisis.
    • Some growth may be genuine demand from economies that kept growing, such as Kazakhstan.
    • Euro values include price increases that differ by product.

    Step 7 · Feedback · 10 min

    A sharp jump, a slow fade

    The three neighbours' line jumps in spring 2022, exactly when sanctions start, peaks in 2023 at nearly twice its pre-war level relative to the rest of the world, and then fades slowly, still about 50% above it in 2026. Kyrgyzstan's line is the most extreme, six to seven times its pre-war level. That pattern fits rerouting: it begins with the sanctions, and it weakens as the EU tightened controls on re-exports from 2023.

    The placebo is not clean: with a fake March 2020 date the neighbours fall about 13 points relative to the rest of the world, because the pandemic hit them harder. The pre-war trend was also drifting down in 2021. If anything, that makes our estimate conservative, but it is the kind of caveat an evaluation note must state.

    Georgia and Uzbekistan, outside the Eurasian Economic Union, also rise: the detour is not limited to Russia's customs-union partners.
