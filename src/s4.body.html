Why does Poland spend so much, and Spain so little?

Regression is the workhorse of quantitative analysis in think tanks and international organisations. In this session you fit a line through European defence spending, read it in plain words, add controls, explain the countries that do not fit, and audit a claim about Poland and Germany.

    Step 1 · Puzzle · 10 min

    The map

    Military spending as a share of GDP in 2024, for 33 European countries. The countries nearest Russia seem to spend the most.

    Your guess

    For each 1,000 km closer to Moscow, how many more points of GDP does a European country spend on defence, on average?

    Step 2 · Lesson · 25 min

    Regression

    A regression line summarises how one variable changes, on average, with another. Its equation has two numbers:

    predicted spending = intercept + slope × distance

    The slope is the answer to the puzzle: how much spending changes for each extra 1,000 km. The intercept is the predicted value at zero distance, mainly useful to place the line.

    Draw the line by eye first

    Move the two sliders until the line runs through the cloud. The number below adds up the squared vertical distances from each country to your line. Least squares chooses the line that makes this number as small as possible.

    –your sum of squared errors
    –least-squares line

    Reading it in words

    A slope of −0.73 reads: “on average, a European country 1,000 km further from Moscow spends 0.73 points of GDP less on defence.” Three things to add every time: on average (individual countries differ), the units, and the sample. The R² says what share of the differences between countries the line accounts for, from 0 to 1.

    Check yourself

    With an intercept of 3.37 and a slope of −0.73, what spending does the line predict for a capital 2,000 km from Moscow (% of GDP, two decimals)?

    Step 4 · Feedback · 10 min

    Spain is not the puzzle

    The line says each 1,000 km closer to Moscow goes with about 0.73 more points of GDP, and distance alone accounts for 38% of the differences (R² = 0.38). Given its distance, Spain is not a low spender at all: it spends 0.6 points more than the line predicts. The real outliers are elsewhere: Poland and Greece far above the line, Austria, Ireland and Switzerland far below.

    Remember

    A residual compares a country with what the line predicts for it, not with the average. “Spends little” and “spends less than expected” are different findings.
    Step 5 · Lesson · 25 min

    Controls, outliers and causes

    Why a coefficient moves

    Distance from Moscow is correlated with other things. Countries near Russia are poorer on average, and poorer countries tend to spend a larger share of GDP. Part of what looked like a distance effect may be an income effect. Adding GDP per capita as a control asks: among countries with the same income, how does spending change with distance?

    spending = a + b₁ × distance + b₂ × GDP per capita + b₃ × NATO member

    If the distance coefficient shrinks when you add a control, part of the simple relationship came from that control.

    What an outlier tells you

    A country far from the line is information. Ask what it has that the model leaves out: a rival other than Russia, a policy of neutrality, a history of occupation, a commitment to NATO's 2% guideline. Often the best next variable comes from explaining the outliers.

    Step 7 · Feedback · 10 min

    Neutrality was the missing variable

    Adding GDP per capita shrinks the distance coefficient from −0.73 to −0.65 and raises R² from 0.38 to 0.55: richer countries spend a slightly smaller share. Adding NATO membership shrinks it again, to −0.56, and R² reaches 0.65. Being in NATO goes with about 0.8 more points of GDP. Austria, Ireland, Switzerland, Malta and Cyprus, all outside NATO, were dragging the line.

    Poland and Greece remain far above the line even with controls. Greece's spending reflects its rivalry with Turkey, a NATO ally; Poland's reflects its frontline position and its history. Neither fits a model built on distance from Moscow alone, and that is a finding, not a failure.
