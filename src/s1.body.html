Can we trust the numbers?

Governments, journalists and think tanks fight with numbers. In this session you learn where those numbers come from, catch an autocracy inflating its growth with satellite images of the Earth at night, and redraw a misleading chart so that it no longer misleads.

    Is Europe disarming or rearming?

    Two commentators look at the same official database of military spending for the 27 countries of today's European Union. One writes “Europe has disarmed since the Cold War”. The other writes “Europe has never spent more on defence”. Here are their charts, both for 1990 to 2021.

    Your call: which headline is true?

    Both are true. Measured in dollars of each year, spending rose from 143 to 262 billion. Measured as a share of the economy, it fell from 2.4% to 1.5% of GDP, because the EU economy grew much faster than its defence budgets. Neither commentator lied: each picked the measure that told the story they wanted. By the end of this session you will know a third measure, spending in real terms, that settles much of the argument.

    Step 2 · Lesson · 25 min

    Where numbers come from

    Every number has a producer

    A country's gross domestic product (GDP) is not observed; it is estimated. A national statistics office combines surveys of firms and households, tax records and assumptions about activity no one records, such as street trading. Defence spending is reported by governments and compiled by researchers, for instance at the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI), which the World Bank republishes. Before using a number, ask who produced it and how.

    Producers have motives

    High growth is a claim to competence. Where officials are promoted for hitting targets and no free press or independent statistics office checks them, the temptation to round up is strong. Governments can also simply stop publishing: after the invasion of Ukraine, Russia stopped releasing detailed foreign-trade statistics in 2022, which made the effect of sanctions harder for outsiders to judge (Carnegie Endowment).

    An outside witness: light at night

    From 1992 to 2013, US military weather satellites photographed the whole Earth at night. Factories, cities, roads and homes emit light, so when an economy grows, it usually gets brighter. No statistics office can edit a satellite image. Economists therefore use the growth of night lights as an independent check on official growth.

    The test

    The idea, from Luis Martínez (Journal of Political Economy, 2022), takes two steps.

    1. In democracies, where official statistics face scrutiny, measure how official growth usually relates to light growth.
    2. Apply that relationship to every country. A country whose official growth sits well above what its light growth implies is a suspect.

    Using the 43 countries rated Free by Freedom House in most years between 1992 and 2013, the relationship is:

    expected official growth = 1.74 + 0.38 × light growth

    Read it in words: a democracy whose lights did not grow at all still grew by about 1.7% a year, and each extra point of light growth went with about 0.38 extra points of GDP growth. The gap is official growth minus expected growth. Martínez found that autocracies' official growth rates are inflated by about 35% on average.

    Light is a witness, not a judge

    • Lights saturate: the centre of a rich city cannot get brighter on the image, so growth there is understated.
    • Some light is not output, such as gas flares at oil fields.
    • Some growth makes little light: software, finance and other services.
    A gap makes a country a suspect worth investigating. It does not prove fraud.

    Check yourself

    A country's lights grew by 5% a year. What official growth would we expect, in % a year?

    Who were they?

    The four largest gaps belong to China, Myanmar, Uzbekistan and Azerbaijan, all rated Not Free. But two cases do not fit the simple story, and they matter more than the four that do.

    • India (E), a democracy, has a large gap. A likely reason is that much of its growth came from services, such as software and call centres, which add output without adding much light.
    • Russia (J), an autocracy, has no gap. Its lights include gas flares, and its official statistics after the 1990s collapse may simply have been accurate on average.

    So the method finds suspects, not culprits. Its strength comes from many countries at once. Here are all 105 countries with more than three million people and usable data:

    On average, autocracies report growth about 0.7 points a year above what their lights imply, roughly a fifth more than the light-implied rate. Martínez's fuller method, which follows each country year by year and controls for many alternative explanations, finds about 35%. Compounded over twenty years, a 0.7-point gap makes an economy look about 15% larger than it is.

    Step 5 · Lesson · 25 min

    Indicators and honest charts

    Back to the puzzle. The same spending can be expressed in at least three ways, and each answers a different question.

    MeasureQuestion it answersTrap
    Current dollarsHow much money was spent that year?Mixes real increases with inflation and exchange-rate swings
    % of GDPHow much effort relative to the size of the economy? NATO's targets use this measure.Can fall while spending rises, if the economy grows faster
    Real termsHow much could the money buy, at constant prices?Depends on the price index and base year chosen

    A useful trick: if you know spending as a share of GDP and GDP in constant prices, you get spending in real terms by multiplying them.

    real spending = (share of GDP ÷ 100) × real GDP

    Five rules for an honest chart

    1. Bars start at zero. A bar's length is its value; cut the axis and you distort the comparison.
    2. Say the unit, and whether values are current or real.
    3. Choose the start year for a reason, and say it. 1990, 2000 and 2014 tell three stories.
    4. Name the source and years.
    5. Write a title the data support, not a slogan.

    Further reading: Claus Wilke, Fundamentals of Data Visualization, chapters 6 and 13 (free online).

    Step 7 · Feedback · 10 min

    Poland, by a distance

    In real terms Poland roughly doubled its defence spending in three years (+104%), from 2.2% to 4.2% of GDP. Germany rose by 46%. Italy barely moved (+3%), and its share of GDP actually fell slightly.

    Now compare with current dollars: Poland +148%, Germany +57%, Italy +5%. The dollar figures overstate every increase because they include rising prices and, for Poland, a stronger zloty. Distance from Russia is part of the explanation, a question you will test properly with a regression in session 4.

    Remember

    Before comparing spending across years, ask: current or real? Before comparing across countries, ask: money or share of GDP? Both questions change rankings.

    Model note

    The chart exaggerates the rise. Its axis starts at 40 billion dollars, so the 2024 bar looks ten times the 2014 bar when spending roughly doubled. It also uses current dollars, which mix real increases with inflation and exchange-rate movements. In constant 2015 dollars, German defence spending rose by about 83% between 2014 and 2024, from 38 to 70 billion, and most of the increase came after 2021. As a share of GDP it rose from 1.1% to 1.9% by SIPRI's measure; NATO's own estimate, which counts more items, put Germany above 2% in 2024. An honest chart starts at zero, shows real values, says so, and names the source (SIPRI, via the World Bank). The story is still one of strong growth, told without exaggeration.
